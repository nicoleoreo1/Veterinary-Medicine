Causes of Fever

Canine Distemper Virus (CDV)

Family: Paramyxoviridae     Genus: Morbillivirus
Transmission: predominantly aerosol (abundant in respiratory secretions), direct contact
Virus shedding: begins 7 days post-infection, lasts 1-2 weeks but can shed up to 60-90 days post-infection
Prevalence: greatest at age 3-6 months, increased risk in unvaccinated/naïve dogs (after loss of maternal antibody)
Clinical signs: generalized → occulonasal or mucopurulent discharge, coughing, lethargy decreased appetite, fever, URI, multi-systemic signs (pneumonia, vomiting, diarrhea, tenesmus, intussusception), chewing gum seizures, cerebellar/vestibular seizures, para-/tetraparesis, ataxia, hyperesthesia, cervical/paraspinal rigidity, myoclonus, nasal and digital hyperkeratosis, pustular dermatitis (uncommon), mild anterior uveitis, optic neuritis, retinal degeneration or detachment, chorioretinitis, keratoconjunctivitis sicca, severe enamel hypoplasia, transplacental infections (abortions, stillbirths, weak puppies, permanent immunodeficiencies, CNS signs in puppies), hypertrophic osteodystrophy, juvenile cellulitis
Pathogenesis: (Day 1) virus multiplies in tissue macrophages and spreads to tonsils and bronchial LNs → (Days 2-4) virus replicates in lymphocytes in tonsil and retropharyngeal and bronchial LNs → (Days 4-6) widespread infection of systemic lymphoid organs, lymphopenia, fever → (Days 8-9) hematogenous spread to epithelial cells and CNS, virus shed in all body secretions
Viremia depends on immune status:
Good cell-mediated immunity and anti-CDV antibodies = clears virus from most tissues
Delayed immune response = virus gradually cleared from tissues but may persist in some tissues (neurons, integument such as foot pads, uveal tract) which leads to delayed clinical signs → CNS signs, hard pad disease, uveitis, optic neuritis, keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eye), chorioretinitis (gold medallion lesions)
No satisfactory immune response = widespread tissue infection with dramatic clinical signs (GI tract, genitourinary tract, respiratory tract, skin, CNS) → virus persists until death
Central nervous system infection: occurs in up to 30% of dogs and is associated with hematogenous spread
Acute CDV encephalitis: occurs in immature or immunocompromised dogs → causes direct viral replication and injury = acute, non-inflammatory demyelination → clinical signs include chewing gum seizures, cerebellar/vestibular seizures, para-/tetraparesis, ataxia, hyperesthesia, cervical/paraspinal rigidity
Subacute and chronic CDV encephalitis: occurs in mature dogs that may have CNS disease without systemic illness → immune reaction (NOT viral replication) leads to inflammatory demyelination = may start with focal signs and progress → clinical signs are similar to acute but include myoclonus
Old dog encephalitis (ODE): very rare and occurs weeks to years after infection in immunocompetent dogs with persistent virus that is replication defective → chronic active and progressive inflammation of cerebrum and brainstem gray matter
Diagnosis: clinical suspicion (full spectrum of signs/more than one organ system, myoclonus), laboratory support (lymphopenia, inclusion bodies), RT-PCR for viral RNA is test of choice, antigen testing (immunofluorescent or immunohistochemistry staining), antibody testing (ELISA IgM for acute clinical cases and ELISA IgG for past or present infection or vaccination), CSF for CNS disease (normal CSF cytology, protein and counts for acute CDV encephalitis and increased protein and cell count due to leakage from blood-CSF barrier and local IgG production for chronic CDV encephalitis)
Therapy: NO CURE → supportive, symptomatic, palliative, treat secondary infections
Prevention: modified live virus CDV vaccine immunity is generally long term → vaccinate q3-4wk between ages 6-16 weeks with a 1 year booster and booster every 3 years after

Feline Coronavirus (FeCoV) and Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP)

FeCoV Type I are the predominant strains worldwide
FeCoV Type II are strains formed from recombination between feline and canine coronaviruses
Transmission: oronasal exposure via feces and fomites
Prevalence: felines ages 3 months to 3 years is most common → any cat carrying any coronavirus is potentially at risk for developing FIP but is a sporadic disease
FeCoV causes GI disease and Feline Infectious Peritonitis (FIP) in cats:
How FeCoV causes FIP: mutation of FeCoV within infected cat allows infection and replication within macrophages → cats are NOT infected with FIP virus → purebred cats are more susceptible, and stress may play a role
Pathogenesis of FIP: Type II & III hypersensitivity (complement-mediated, antibody-antigen) – macrophage tropism of FeCoV to transform to FIP → infected macrophages attach to vessels and the virus invades tissues → systemic pyogranulomatous vasculitis
Produce antibodies but an inadequate cell-mediated immunity → develop effusive "wet form" of FIP = exudation of plasma proteins – yellow color from RBC breakdown products (bilirubin and biliverdin), RBC damage from vasculitis, clotting and complement factor activation, DIC
Produce antibodies and partially protective cell-mediated immunity → develop non-effusive "dry form" of FIP = granuloma/pyogranuloma formation
Produce antibodies and aggressive cell-mediated immunity → less likely to develop FIP but may harbor latent virus for years that may reactivate if immunosuppressed or co-infected with FeLV
Clinical signs of FIP: vague (lethargy, anorexia, weight loss, chronic fever) and varied (bloated abdomen, blindness, neurologic signs, diarrhea, vomiting)
Clinical signs of "wet form" FIP: high protein fluid accumulation in body cavities (modified transudate to exudate – high protein, low cell count) caused by immune complex damage to vessels, abdominal fluid, pleural fluid present in 25%
Clinical signs of "dry form" FIP: pyogranulomatous lesions (neutrophils and macrophages) commonly in eyes, CNS and lymph nodes, clinical signs due to organ dysfunction (blindness, seizures, ataxia, liver failure, kidney failure)
Clinical signs of ocular FIP: usually with non-effusive form – anterior uveitis, chorioretinitis, keratic precipitates, hyphema, hypopyon, retinal detachment and hemorrhage
Diagnosis: laboratory findings
Complete blood count (CBC) – nonspecific and variable → anemia, lymphopenia
Serum chemistry – hyperglobulinemia (effusive and non-effusive forms), hypoalbuminemia with albumin:globulin < 0.4 highly predictive of FIP (rule out, not rule in – esp. > 0.8), hyperbilirubinemia
Protein electrophoresis – polyclonal gammopathy (high gamma globulin level)
Abdominal +/- pleural effusion – tends to be high protein (tenacious, straw-colored, clear or slightly cloudy, +/- fibrin clots), non-septic exudate (protein >3.5 g/dl) or moderate cellularity modified transudate (<5000 cells/uL)
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) – absolute gold standard for detecting virus in tissues for FIP diagnosis – antigen is within tissue macrophages (often post-mortem diagnosis) → only cats with FIP have positive tests
Immunofluorescence (or immunocytochemistry) – detection of FeCoV in macrophages in effusion → high positive predictive value but poor negative predictive value
Immunofluorescent antibody (IFA) ELISA – gold standard for FeCoV antibody detection → does not test for FIP antibody – a positive antibody test does not confirm FIP and a negative test does not rule out FIP
RT-PCR – highly sensitive and specific for FIP infection in abdominal or thoracic effusion but low sensitivity for peripheral blood mononuclear cells and serum
RT-nestedPCR – PCR for FeCoV and 2 mutations in the spike gene in which 1 of the 2 mutations are present in >98% of FIP viruses detected in diseased tissue
Cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) – pleocytosis (lots of cells) often marked (20-10,000 cells/uL – neutrophilic), high protein (30 to >1000 mg/dL), coronavirus antibody titer may correlate with FIP but CSF can be normal in FIP cats with neuro signs
Diagnosis: pathologic findings
Gross pathologic findings – effusions (peritoneal, pericardial, pleural), mesenteric adhesions, granulomas (large or miliary), enlarged lymph nodes, fibrinous exudate +/- ventricular dilation in brain
Histopathologic findings – systemic perivascular granulomatous or pyogranulomatous lesions, multifocal, coalescing
Therapy: INCURABLE? (new medications hold promise – nucleoside analog GS-441524 which is a metabolite of the antiviral drug remdesivir → results in premature termination of viral RNA synthesis) → supportive therapy with fluids and probiotics, prednisone (suppression of immune-complex component but not curative – 2-4 mg/kg SID)
Prognosis: 84-90% survival rates with injectable GS-441524, 77-83% survival rate with oral antivirals → medial survival of 9-38 days without medications → negative prognostic indicators include hyperbilirubinemia, lymphopenia, large volumes of effusion
Prevention: queen in isolated and disinfected rooms, test queen before kittens are born (if positive then remove kittens from mom at 4-6 weeks before waning maternal antibodies occurs), test kittens after 10 weeks for seroconversion

Feline Leukemia Virus (FeLV)

Retrovirus inserted into the genome
Transmission: vertically from infected queens to kittens and horizontally among cats that live together or fight → virus shed in saliva, nasal secretions, feces, milk, and urine → transmission primarily through grooming
Prevalence: at-risk cats include cats that are unvaccinated and have outdoor access, males, sick, have bite wounds, oral disease, known exposure (30 days), live in multi-cat environments, enter shelters or rescues, are newly acquired cats or kittens, and prior to receiving FeLV vaccination → seroprevalence of FeLV is 2.5%
Clinical signs: fever, lymphadenopathy, leukopenia
Pathogenesis: virus replicates in draining lymph nodes → virus spreads through lymphoid tissues and bone marrow → virus infects epithelial cells to be shed in secretions
Outcomes of FeLV infection – determined by age, immune system, infectious pressure, virus virulence
Abortive infection – infection is cleared prior to viremia (rare) → never p27 antigen positive
Regressive infection – transient viremia, then proviral DNA inserts into host genome and may never cause disease again (does not shed virus) but reactivation is possible → p27 antigen positive in initial viremic period but negative by 16 weeks
Progressive infection – persistent viremia that develops into FeLV-associated diseases such as neoplasia (lymphoma, leukemia, fibrosarcoma), opportunistic infections (Mycoplasma hemofelis), pure red cell aplasia (FeLV C), aplastic anemia, myelodysplasia, or myelofibrosis, immune-mediated disease (IMHA, ITP, GN, IMPA, uveitis), peripheral lymphadenopathy, neurologic disease, reproductive failure, GI disease (uncommon) → p27 antigen ELISA remains positive, IFA positive
Diagnosis: all at-risk cats should be tested and potential blood donors
Screening test – soluble core antigen p27 detected via ELISA → negative p27 ELISA could indicate no infection or regressive infection
Confirmatory test – immunofluorescent antibody assay (IFA), second ELISA or proviral DNA PCR (high titers may indicate progressive disease, low titers are consistent with regressive infection)
Management: keep infected cat indoors, vaccinate with core vaccines, at least twice yearly veterinary visits and lab work, test and vaccinate housemates
Therapy: treat secondary infections, treat lymphoma with chemotherapy, treat anemia with erythropoietin analogs – antivirals and immune stimulants have shown no proven effect
Prevention: test new cats prior to entry into household or shelter, vaccinate kittens and at 1 year with further vaccination dependent on risks, isolate infected cats from unexposed cats, keep cats indoors

Feline Immunodeficiency Virus (FIV)

Retrovirus inserted into the genome
Transmission: via bite wounds (high concentration in saliva) – horizontally among cats that live together is uncommon
Prevalence: increased risk cats include free-roaming adults and males and at-risk cats include sick cats, cats with bite wounds, oral disease, known exposure (30 days), in multi-cat environments, cats entering shelters or rescue, newly acquired cats and kittens and prior to FIV vaccination → 18.2% of sick feral cats are FIV+
Clinical signs: fever, malaise, diarrhea
Pathogenesis: many cats remain asymptomatic and have a normal to moderately decreased lifespan and few cats have progressive infection
Progressive FIV – progression is related to virus strain and host immunity with most cats dying within 1 year once ill → outcomes include opportunistic infections, chronic gingivostomatitis, chronic rhinitis, immune-mediated disease (GN), peripheral lymphadenopathy, weight loss, lymphoma, squamous cell carcinoma, neuropathy, seizures, behavior change (neurovirulent strains)
Diagnosis:
Screening test – antibody tests via ELISA (high sensitivity) – serology does not differentiate vaccinal antibodies → kittens less than 6 months with positive results may be maternal antibody and not true infection
Positive ELISA → retest kitten after 6 months and do viral PCR in vaccinated cats
Negative ELISA but suspected → viral PCR
Management: keep indoors, neuter or spay, vaccinate with core vaccines, at least twice-yearly veterinary visits and lab work, test housemates
Therapy: treat secondary infections, treat lymphoma with chemotherapy, erythropoietin in cats with severe non-regenerative anemia, antivirals may be helpful (AZT can cause bone marrow suppression), human alpha interferon

Leptospirosis

Leptospira interogans: thin, motile, spirochetes found in contaminated water and carried by rodents and other wildlife species (ie. raccoons)
Serovars known to affect dogs: Canicola, Icterohemorrhagiae, Grippotyphosa, Pomona, Bratislava, Harjo, Ballum, Autumnalis, Australis → pathogenic and non-pathogenic serovars are maintained in nature by numerous sub clinically affected host species
Leptospirosis in cats: less susceptible than dogs but exposure causes seroconversion to positive antibody titers → usually mild or unapparent clinically and there may be histologic lesions in kidneys and liver
Organism survives in moisture and moderately warm temperatures → organisms can be viable for months in soil saturated by urine → most prevalent in late summer and early fall in upper Midwest during high rainfall season
Transmission: direct (urine, venereal, placental, bite wounds, ingestion of infected material or tissues) and indirect (contaminated water, food, soil and bedding from wildlife, infected pets, and farm animals)
Incubation period of 1-7 days with replication in blood starting 1 day post infection
Prevalence: large and small breeds less than 15 pounds (Yorkies), dogs that have direct exposure to wildlife and engage in outdoor activities such as hunting and swimming
Clinical signs: fever (103-104 F), malaise, vomiting, dehydration, icterus, peripheral vascular collapse, hematemesis, hematochezia, epistaxis, petechial hemorrhages (vasculitis), other effects (benign meningitis, uveitis, abortion, infertility)
Clinical presentation: acute kidney injury (failure), hepatic disease (typically with icterus), combo of kidney and liver (high index suspicion for lepto), polyuria/polydipsia
Leptospirosis pulmonary hemorrhage (LPH) or lepto lung – vasculitis causing fluid exudation from lungs, pulmonary hemorrhage due to effect of toxins on the lungs → regional to California (up to 70% of cases)
Pathogenesis: leptospires penetrate intact mucous membranes or abraded, scratched or water-softened skin → replication in blood causing vasculitis → spread to organs
Pathogenicity varies among serovars
Renal but more hepatic involvement – Icterohemorrhagiae, Pomona
Predominantly renal – Canicola and Grippotyphosa
Renal colonization – organism replicated and persists in renal tubular epithelial cells → 2 weeks postinfection the organism is present in tubular cells and tubular lumen → onset of shedding in urine and intermittent shedding occurs even with clinical recovery
Hepatic injury – lepto LPS interferes with biliary processing and hepatic injury may occur without structural hepatic lesions through subcellular damage caused by leptospira → amount of icterus correlated with hepatic necrosis
Chronic active hepatitis occurs with the Grippotyphosa serovar – extensive fibrosis and dysfunction may occur = canines with hepatic dysfunction or fibrosis should be tested for leptospirosis
Diagnosis: laboratory findings
Complete blood count (CBC) – leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia
Serum chemistry – typical renal findings of acute kidney injury (azotemia, impaired urine concentration) and typical hepatic findings (acute – subacute hepatitis with increased liver enzyme activities of ALP >ALT and hyperbilirubinemia)
Detect antibodies directed at bacteria –
Microscopic agglutination test (MAT) – quantitative antibody acute (day 1) and convalescent (7-14 days) titers of common serovars (6-8) → titer may be false negative or ≤ 1:800 during first 7 to 10 days and needs a second test → titer ≥ 1:1600 is hx of vaccination or likely leptospirosis and titer ≥ 1:6400 is leptospirosis dx → ≥ 4-fold increase in paired (acute and convalescent) titers confirms dx
SNAP Lepto (Idexx) and WITNESS Lepto Rapid Test (Zoetis) – performed in clinic on blood and or urine before antimicrobial administration *rapid test that does not require live leptospirosis cultures
Detect bacteria directly –
Bacterial DNA by PCR in blood or urine – bacteremic phase first 7-10 days after infection and bacteriuric phase after 1st week → test both blood and urine before beginning antimicrobial therapy (recent vx does not interfere with PCR) → positive result and clinical signs suggests lepto but a negative result does not rule-out lepto
Culture
Darkfield microscopy
Diagnosis: imaging
Abdominal ultrasound – renomegaly, pyelectasia, increased cortical echogenicity
Thoracic radiographs – interstitial to nodular alveolar densities → lepto lungs
Therapy: parenteral penicillins for acute treatment eliminates leptospiremia (stops active infection and active organ injury and probably stops urinary shedding) but does not resolve carrier state → switch to 2 weeks doxycycline (eliminates carrier state) or continue penicillins (ampicillin or amoxicillin or penicillin G) if patient cannot tolerate doxycycline due to GI signs → supportive care with fluid therapy, antiemetics, GI protectants, phosphate binders, hepatic support medications, nutritional support
Prognosis: most survive (~80%) with therapy beginning early enough, kidney failure or severe respiratory complications results in poorer prognosis, monitoring is vital as may lead to chronic renal and hepatic diseases
Prevention: vaccination – bacterins against Canicola, Icterohemorrhagiae, Pomona and Grippotyphos → 2 injections given 2-4 weeks apart gives 12+ months of shedding and prevents renal shedding

Rabies Virus

Family: Rhabdoviridae     Genera: Lyssavirus
Structure: non-segmented negative-sense ssRNA virus – bullet shaped, enveloped, large glycoprotein spikes, G protein is target of neutralizing antibodies, easily destroyed by heat/sunlight/drying/antiseptics and can remain viable in carcass for a few days at 20 Celsius
Transmission: saliva of an infected animal is most common source and viral shedding in saliva can occur before clinical signs become apparent, mammals are vectors and reservoirs in nature
Incubation period varies – 1-6 months in dogs and cats, 1-12 months in humans and longer than 2 years in rare cases
Prevalence: infects all warm-blooded animals and in nearly all animals the infection is fatal → cats are identified more often than dogs in the USA
Species vary in level of susceptibility – high (bats, skunks, raccoons, cattle), moderate (dogs, cats, sheep, goats, horses, monkeys), and low (birds, primitive mammals such as opossum)
Rabies is endemic in skunks in the upper Midwest.
Clinical signs: change in mental status, excessive drooling of saliva, nerve-muscular paralysis
Pathogenesis: virus replicates in the muscle (limited) → virus buds into the synaptic cleft of the neuro muscular junction via viral attachment with the G glycoproteins → virus is taken up by the neurons and begins retrograde axonal transport to CNS and reaches the limbic system and neocortex in the brain (virus has no preference for neurons in a specific brain region) → virus travels to neuronal body (soma) to replicate → virus moves anterograde via peripheral nerves from CNS to salivary glands, adrenal cortex, and pancreas → virus enters acinar cells of salivary glands and buds at apical surface of mucous cells to release virus in saliva
Virus is hidden from immune response and is highly neurotropic with no viremic phase
Clinical phases –
Prodromal (CNS viral spread) – premonitory sign indicating the onset of disease that is 2-3 days in dogs and 1-2 days in cats → mood changes (apprehension, nervousness, solitude, friendly animals turn shy and fractious animals turn docile and affectionate), variable fever, pupils dilated, slow palpebral/corneal reflex, lick at bite site, pruritis
Furious (forebrain involvement) – common stage in affected cats where the virus replicates in the limbic system of the brain → restlessness, excitable, roaming, aggressive, vicious, photophobic, hyperesthetic, pica, incoordination, disorientation, seizures
Paralytic (apoptosis) – occurs usually within 10 days of other signs but may occur early or alone and is a more common phase seen in dogs in which the virus has reached the neocortex of the brain → earliest lower motor neuron signs occur near site of entry and progress (weakness, paralysis), laryngeal paralysis (altered bark, drooling, frothing at mouth, hydrophobia), dropped jaw → death usually within 2-4 days
Death
Diagnosis: clinical suspicion (clinical signs, vaccination history of not vaccinated, known/potential exposure from bite wound or dog fight)
Rabies should be considered in any animal that suddenly develops profound behavior changes or features of low motor neuron paralysis or both, especially in the unvaccinated.
Diagnosis: laboratory findings
Histopathologic exam – negri bodies (intracytoplasmic inclusions or aggregates of virus nucleocapsids) and perivascular cuffing → negri bodies are rarely developed in cats and has a poor diagnostic yield
Direct fluorescent antibody test – primary test (rapid, sensitive, reproducible) for rabies in animals that bite humans → performed on fresh or refrigerated bilateral nervous tissue (brain) including the hippocampus, cerebellum, and medulla oblongata → all animals with virus in saliva should be positive
Prevention: all dogs, cats, and ferrets should be vaccinated (no modified live available) by a licensed veterinarian at 3 months of age and no earlier due to inference of maternal antibodies → immunity is conferred in approx. 28 days → vaccinate again 1 year later and either annual or triennial thereafter depending on labeling of the product used
Rabies certificate must include, ink signature or verifiable digital signature of license veterinarian, name/address/telephone number of owner, pet's name/breed/size/sex/age/species/color, vaccine name/manufacturer/serial number/expiration date/duration of immunity, rabies tag number, date the vaccine was administered, name/address/license number of veterinarian who administered or supervised the administration of the vaccine, and due date of the next rabies vaccination

Mycobacterium

Agent: aerobic, nonmotile, non-spore-forming, pleomorphic rod bacteria – intracellular pathogen
Mycobacterium is more resistant to disinfection than many other pathogens
Clinical manifestation is based on three primary factors –
1. Route of infection
Pulmonary Mycobacterium – pneumonia, pulmonary mass lesions (granulomas), tracheobronchial lymphadenopathy, pleural or pericardial effusion, pyothorax → clinical signs include cough, tachypnea, dyspnea, and cyanosis
GI Mycobacterium – granulomatous lesions, palpable mesenteric lymph nodes, abdominal effusion → clinical signs include weight loss, anorexia or hyporexia, vomiting, and diarrhea
Cutaneous Mycobacterium – dermal nodules, non-healing wounds with draining tracts, ulcerative lesions → common lesion sites include face, abdomen, extremities, tail base, perineum, ventral thorax, and can occur as oropharyngeal lesions, CNS disease, uveitis, chorioretinitis, musculoskeletal lesions, hepatic disease (icterus), and more
2. Host immune response – Mycobacterium infection sets off a complicated inflammatory cascade – macrophages are stimulated to invade tissue → cytokine production drives the recruitment of neutrophils and mononuclear cells → granuloma formation
Mycobacterium can cause a type IV hypersensitivity (cell-mediated reaction) or delayed type hypersensitivity
Defects in host immunity increases risk for disseminated disease and bacteremia
3. Type of Mycobacterium
Classify based culture growth – difficult to grow, slow growing (often more severe and disseminated disease), and fast growing
Tubercle formation – tubercle-forming (M. tuberculosis, M. bovis, M. microtti) and non-TB (M. avium complex – MAC)
Leprosy-causing Mycobacterium – M. lepraemurium can cause leproid granulomas and is difficult to grown in culture, often quickly progressive, locally spreading, cutaneous/subcutaneous nodules
Transmission: inhalation, ingestion, and contact or inoculation
Prevalence: M. tuberculosis in humans, dogs, rarely cats; M. bovis in cattle, dogs, cats; M. microtti in small rodents, cats; Miniature Schnauzers and Basset Hounds have an increased risk for M. avium complex
Clinical signs: localized or disseminated dermal lesions (nodules, ulcerated lesions, draining tracts), lymphadenopathy, fever, granulomatous/pyogranulomatous lesions (especially respiratory, GI, skin, and other – can be multiple systems)
Diagnosis:
Culture – not all species of Mycobacterium grow in culture and many take a long time to grow (months) → need specialized laboratories
PCR – provides faster results but availability of testing can be an issue – fresh tissue samples are preferred
Cytology or histopathology – Mycobacterium has mycolic acid in cell wall = acid fast bacteria that stains clear in Diff-Quik and needs acid fast stains such as Ziehl-Neelsen for visualization → culture of PCR can help identify species
Therapy: prolonged (2-6 months) and requires a combination of antibiotic therapy (Rifampin, Fluoroquinolone, Macrolide) with potential surgical excision of lesions → treatment should be discussed with owner at the start of treatment
Prognosis: highly variable and depends on disease severity at time of diagnosis, immune status of the patient, species of bacteria, and location of infection – it can be difficult to truly clear infection
Zoonotic potential: caution with immunocompromised clients, the very young, or the very old

Bartonella

Agent: gram negative, pleomorphic, fastidious (difficult to grow in culture), facultative intracellular bacteria – Alphaproteobacteria (related to Brucella, Rickettsia, Anaplasma, Wolbachia, etc.)
Species most medically important for dogs and cats – Bartonella henselae, Bartonella vinsonii subsp. Berkhoffii, Bartonella koehlerae
Clinical manifestations: acute infections are likely self-limiting and highly variable
Cardiovascular – endocarditis (primarily affecting aortic valve and mitral valve), myocarditis, vasculitis, vasoproliferative diseases → consider Bartonella with aortic valve endocarditis + negative cultures
Intermittent (relapsing) fever
Granulomatous disease – lymphadenitis, rhinitis, hepatitis
Polyarthritis, uveitis, neurologic disease, epistaxis, and more
Bartonella in cats is slightly different – less pathogenic in cats than in dogs but FeLV may predispose to B. henselae infection or persistence
Transmission: vector borne disease (arthropod species are growing), other modes possible (stercorarian transmission, transfusions, vertical transmission, needle sticks) → Bartonella is stealthy (extensive mechanisms evade or suppress host immune response) and colonized RBCs and endothelial cells without causing hemolysis to be preserved for vector transmission, protected from immune system and contribute to decreased antibiotic efficacy
Cats are a reservoir host for B. henselae and B. koehlerae = can be subclinically infected for months or years
Clinical signs: endocarditis (aortic valve, culture negative), granulomatous/pyogranulomatous inflammation, vasculitis/vascular pathology, chronic relapsing fevers, partial antimicrobial response or worsening with immune suppression, history of arthropod exposures
Diagnosis:
Complete blood count – thrombocytopenia (+/- immune-mediated – cats and dogs), anemia (+/- immune-mediated – cats and dogs), neutrophilia or neutropenia (cats and dogs), eosinophilia (cats and dogs), monocytosis
Serum chemistry – hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia (cats and dogs)
Serology – poorly sensitive due to Bartonella's ability to evade the immune system and positive serology could indicate past exposure or non-active infection in healthy dogs (chronic infections) → 4-fold increase in serologic titers observed over 2-3 weeks indicates seroconversion and active infection but titers decrease rapidly (3-6 months) with treatment
PCR – false negatives are common due to low levels of organisms and cyclic bacteremia and previous antibiotics can increase chance for false negatives – positive PCR in sick dogs can suggest true infection
Culture – Bartonella is fastidious and slow-growing = requires specific nutrients in growth media
Bartonella Alphaproteobacteria Growth Medium (BAPGM) – culture samples are incubated in specialized liquid culture medium for 7 days, then followed by PCR assay → most sensitive diagnostic (recommend a triple draw)
Cytology or histopathology – even with severe inflammatory lesions, the bacteria can be hard to visualize → special stains or immunohistochemistry can be used to increase sensitivity
Co-infections are possible, so screening for other vector-borne diseases is recommended
Serology and PCR are both often submitted
Therapy: optimal protocol not yet established, but doxycycline plus a fluoroquinolone is recommended → long duration of antibiotic may be necessary (4-6 weeks) to eliminate infection if bacteremia is persistent
Some species of Bartonella can develop rapid resistance to macrolides like azithromycin
Prognosis: highly variable and depends on disease severity at time of diagnosis, immune status of the patient, species of bacteria, and location of infection – it can be difficult to truly clear infection
Prevention: flea/tick prevention, indoor lifestyle for cats is preferable to reduce risk of flea infestation
Zoonosis potential: Bartonella henselae is the causative agent of "cat scratch disease"

Mycoplasma haemofelis

Agent: small, pleomorphic, gram-negative (but does not have cell wall) bacteria (cocci, rings, rods) that can form short (3-8) chains and lives on surface of RBCs (epicellular or epierythrocytic) – causes hemolytic anemia
Major species that infect cats – Mycoplasma haemofelis (causative agent of feline infectious anemia), Candidatus Mycoplasma haemominutum, and Candidatus Mycoplasma turcinesis
Transmission: biting and aggressive interactions, fleas and other arthropods, vertical transmission, blood transfusion (PCR screening for blood donors)
Prevalence: hemotropic Mycoplasmas (hemoplasmas) infect a wide range of species – Mycoplasma haemofelis is most common in male, non-pedigree, older cats with outdoor access
Clinical signs: pale mucous membranes, tachycardia, fever, weakness, lethargy, inappetence
Diagnosis:
Complete blood count – regenerative anemia (often severe)
Serum chemistry – +/- hyperbilirubinemia (if present, likely mild), +/- bilirubinuria (if hyperbilirubinemic), +/- elevated ALT
FeLV/FIV test – studies are conflicting but may have shared risk factors/exposures especially for FIV and cats with immune compromise from concurrent viral infection may have more severe disease → test for FeLV/FIV
PCR – sensitive, specific, and can help differentiate from other hemoplasmas but is not always needed – submit prior to antibiotics if possible → PCR may be false negative if cat received antibiotics before submission, Mycoplasma numbers in blood are low and fluctuate
Cytology – alone has poor sensitivity (can get false negatives) so have a pathologist review → cytology may be false positive if stain precipitate or Howell-Jolly bodies were falsely identified as Mycoplasma in RBCs = keep stain fresh and send a blood smear with EDTA tube if sending sample out for cytology
Culture – hemoplasmas cannot be cultured
Therapy: due to Mycoplasma not having a cell wall, antibiotics that target cell walls like beta-lactams should not be administered → first stabilize patient → administer doxycycline for at least 2 weeks as first-line treatment (follow doxycycline administration with food or water to encourage complete swallowing to prevent esophageal stricture in cats) → fluoroquinolone is considered the second line choice
Corticosteroids are not typically indicated
Prognosis: anemia may improve, but clearance of infection is difficult, and reactivation of infection can occur → prognosis is generally good if immune competent and prompt therapy
Prevention: flea and tick prevention, reduce outdoor lifestyle

Rickettsial Diseases

Ticks

Questing behavior: ticks detect animals' breath and body odors or by sensing body heat, moisture, and vibrations
Transovarial transmission: infection passed from arthropod parents to offspring
Transstadial transmission: infection passed from one life stage to the next (larva → nymph → adult)

Family Anaplasmataceae (Anaplasma spp., Ehrlichia spp., Neorickettsia spp., Rickettsia spp.)

Common features: Gram – obligate intracellular bacteria forming morulae and infecting leukocytes, erythrocytes, and platelets but require arthropod vector
Life cycle: tick attaches to host for 24-48 hours → bacteria is transferred from the tick salivary glands into the subcutaneous tissues of the host → bacteria within dense core cells bind to surface of the host cell → the infectious dense core cell enters the host cell via receptor mediated endocytosis forming a phagolysosome → formation of phagolysosome results in morulae formation with replication via binary fission to produce dense core cells → dense core cells are liberated from the cells by exocytosis or cell lysis → incubation period of 1-2 weeks in the dog
Therapy: doxycycline will treat all
Prevention: tick control

Order Rickettsiales

Canine monocytic erlichiosis
Etiology: Ehrlichia canis
Prevalence: worldwide distribution → disease in dogs
Tick vector: Rhipicephalus sanguineus (brown dog tick) – 3 host tick
Cell tropism: monocytes
Pathogenesis: 3 stages with thrombocytopenia in all stages!
Acute stage (1-4 weeks long) – 8-20 day incubation with replication of organism in reticuloendothelial tissues (splenomegaly, lymphadenopathy) → vasculitis → platelet disfunction and immune-mediated thrombocytopenia → recover in 2-4 weeks and clear or become carrier
Subclinical stage (months to years long) – sequestration of organism in spleen → antigenic variation to evade hose immune response → recover or become chronic
Chronic stage – severe disease – pancytopenia, bone marrow hypoplasia, marked granular lymphocytosis and bone marrow plasmacytosis with monoclonal gammopathy can lead to misdiagnosis of leukemia or plasmacytoma → death due to hemorrhage and secondary infections
Clinical signs: lethargy, inappetence, fever, lameness, weight loss, epistaxis, petechia or ecchymoses, neuro signs, hyphema, dyspnea
Diagnosis: thrombocytopenia (mild in subclinical phase), pancytopenia in chronic phase with some moderate to marked granular lymphocytosis, hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia (usually polyclonal but can be monoclonal), increased clotting times → 100% probability of death with severe leukopenia, severe anemia, and increased aPTT
IFA (immunofluorescent antibody) = gold standard and can be elevated for over a year
Idexx SNAP 4Dx
PCR = performed before antibiotics and best in acute phase but useful in all
Canine granulocytic erlichiosis
Etiology: Ehrlichia ewingii
Prevalence: Oklahoma, Arkansas, Missouri, Kansas → disease in dogs, humans, and goats
Tick vector: Amblyomma americanum (lone star tick) – 3 host tick
Cell tropism: granulocytes, primarily neutrophils
Pathogenesis: only acute disease with 3-4 week long incubation period
Clinical signs: may be clinically unapparent, fever, lethargy, anorexia → neutrophilic polyarthritis
Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever
Etiology: Rickettsia rickettsii
Prevalence: disease in dogs and humans
Tick vector: Dermacentor spp. (American dog tick) – 3 host tick
Cell tropism: endothelial cells
Pathogenesis: transfer of organism in hours → endotheliotropism results in vasculitis
Clinical signs: abnormal hemostasis, edema, microthrombosis, hypovolemia, petechiae, hypoalbuminemia, thrombosis, and necrosis
Diagnosis: culture, PCR, tissue IFA, convalescent serology
Public health: reportable in humans and in dogs in some states and counties, dogs and people can be infected by ticks in same environment (transovarial transmission), needle sticks pose a risk
Canine cyclic thrombocytopenia
Etiology: Anaplasma platys
Prevalence: worldwide distribution → disease in dogs
Tick vector: Rhipicephalus sanguineus (brown dog tick) – 3 host tick
Cell tropism: thrombocytes (platelets)
Pathogenesis: 1-2 week incubation period → 2-3 weeks post infection is platelet nadir that normalize in 3-4 weeks → cycles of bacteremia and thrombocytopenia every 1-2 weeks
Clinical signs: sometimes lethargy, fever, lymphadenopathy, uveitis, mucosal hemorrhages
Diagnosis: thrombocytopenia
Canine granulocytic anaplasmosis
Etiology: Anaplasma phagocytophilum
Prevalence: worldwide distribution → disease in dogs, cats, humans, and horses
Tick vector: Ixodes scapularis of Midwest and East Coast and Ixodes pacificus of West Coast (blacklegged tick or deer tick) – 3 host tick
Cell tropism: granulocytes, primarily neutrophils → morulae indistinguishable from Ehrlichia ewingii
Pathogenesis: acute disease with 1-2 week incubation period
Clinical signs: most dogs are not clinical – febrile illness often self-limiting, lameness, lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, bleeding due to coinfections
Diagnosis: thrombocytopenia in 90% of dogs, neutrophilia or neutropenia, mild ALP elevations, morulae may appear in neutrophils 4-8 days PI
Convalescent serology – antibodies may persist for months and 25-40% of clinical dogs are antibody negative at time of diagnosis
PCR – before antibiotic administration

Lyme Disease

Etiology: Borrelia burgforferi sensu stricto – motile spirochete
Tick vector: Ixodes scapularis of Midwest and East Coast and Ixodes pacificus of West Coast (blacklegged tick or deer tick) – 3 host tick
Prevalence: most commonly reported vector-born disease in humans in the US; 6.5-85% seroprevalence in dogs in endemic areas (85% of cases in eastern coastal states MA/VA, 10% of cases in MN/WI, 4% of cases in northern CA)
10% of MN dogs tested were positive for Lyme C6 antibodies → 5-10% of seropositive dogs develop clinical Lyme disease → <1-2% develop Lyme nephritis
Transmission: outer surface proteins (Osp) – ticks express OspA in which the spirochete is within the tick midgut before taking a blood meal → expression of OspC and movement of the spirochete into the tick salivary glands are induced during the tick taking a blood meal → about 48 hours of tick attachment
Pathogenesis: synovial tissue (macrophages, T cells, B cells, plasma cells) and joint fluid (NEUTROPHILS, immune complexes, complement, inflammatory Th1 cytokines), glomerulonephritis in young dogs (antibody-antigen complexes, causes protein loss in urine – albumin, antithrombin III)
Clinical signs:
Polyarthropathy – develops 2-5 months after tick bite, fever, inappetence, lameness affecting one to multiple joints with shifting leg lameness (first joint effected is closest to tick bite)
Lyme nephritis – 30% have concurrent polyarthritis, weight loss, vomiting, polyuria, polydipsia
Physical exam findings: fever, lameness, joint effusion common, generalized lymphadenopathy; findings indicating Lyme nephritis include thin BCS, dehydration, peripheral edema, pleural effusion, ascites, hypertension, and retinal detachment
Diagnosis:
Synovial fluid – marked numbers of nondegenerate neutrophils (>5000 and often >10,000 cells/ul)
Serology – VlsE (variable major protein-like expressed) is an antigenically carriable cell-surface protein on the organism that evades the antibody response by continuously changing its surface epitopes → C6 is an invariable region of the VlsE
OspC in early infection, OspF in chronic infection (correlates with C6), OspA in vaccination
Idexx 4Dx Plus ELISA – detects C6 protein → false negatives are rare, no cross reaction with vaccine
Idexx QuantC6 ELISA – detects C6 protein → no correlation with disease severity
Zoetis VetScan Lyme Rapid Test – detects diff. VlsE lipoprotein, OspC, and p41 (flagellin) → new test with little information on validation
Western blot – detects B. burgdorferi antigens → technically difficult but can distinguish natural infection, vaccination or both
Multiplex fluorescent bead – detects OspA, OspC, and OspF → sensitivity, specificity in natural infection unknown
Antech Accuplex 4 – detects OspA, OspC, OspF, SLP, and P39 → less sensitive and specific for B. burgdorferi and 4Dx
Urinalysis – for all seropositive dogs → proteinuria present before onset of clinical signs is associated with Lyme nephritis → consider renal biopsy for suspect Lyme nephritis cases early in the course of disease to identify immune-complexes in the glomerular basement membrane (NOT Borrelia specific)
Treatment: doxycycline (5-10 mg/kg PO BID x 28 d) or amoxicillin (20 mg/kg PO TID x 28 d) → most dogs with Lyme arthropathy respond to treatment in 1-2 days → additional therapy for Lyme nephritis includes immunosuppressives, anti-hypertensives, angiotensin receptor blockers, anti-thrombotics
Chronic or additional antibiotics are unlikely to be necessary and unlikely to be effective
Prevention: flea-tick preventatives 12 months a year, tick removal within 24 hours, vaccination

Plague

Etiology: Yersinia pestis, Gram – nonmotile, coccobacillus, "safety pin" appearance → causative agent of Black Death
Sources: infection maintained by the flea-rodent-flea cycle – prairie dogs, rock squirrels and ground squirrels are common wild hosts
Persistence: sensitive to desiccation and heat >40 Celsius, persists weeks to months in organic material, may persist for years in cold temperatures or freezing temperatures
Prevalence: occurs in the US (Southwestern states, Colorado, New Mexico, Arizona) in semiarid, cooler climates adjacent to deserts any time of year but mostly late spring to early fall → cats are highly susceptible like humans, and dogs develop only mild clinical signs with seroconversion
Transmission: bites from infected fleas, contact with mucous membranes or broken skin, droplets from animals with pneumonic plague
Pathogenesis:
Three clinical forms:
Bubonic plague (most common and least lethal) – acquired by rodent ingestion and may spread and progress to septicemic or pneumonic phase → clinical signs include fever, dehydration, submandibular lymphadenitis, hyperesthesia
Septicemic plague (with or without bubo formation) – hematogenous spread may result in virtually every organ being involved but is most common in the lungs of cats → clinical signs include septic shock (anorexia, tachycardia, vomiting, diarrhea, weak pulse, hypotension, leukocytosis) → typically fatal within 1-2 days
Pneumonic plague – caused by inhalation of infectious droplets or from septicemic plague and is a significant risk to owners, veterinarians, and others in close contact due to aerosolization of bacteria → clinical signs similar to septicemic plague with pulmonary involvement
Diagnosis: consider plague in any febrile cat in or traveled to endemic areas! → collect samples (whole blood, lymph node aspirates, swabs from draining lesions, oropharyngeal swabs) then begin antimicrobial therapy before diagnosis is confirmed → avoid contact with purulent material and submit culture samples and fixed slides to appropriate authorities (contact health department)
Gram stain – monomorphic population of Gram – organisms with bipolar "safety pin" morphology
Serology – two samples 10-14 days apart and need 4-fold increase to confirm diagnosis → titers commonly persist over 1 year after exposure in endemic areas
Thoracic radiographs
Treatment: isolate patient for 48-72 hours or longer if pneumonic and handle patients with PPE, examine for and eliminate fleas immediately if found, lance buboes and flush with chlorhexidine, handle wastes from flushes appropriately
Antimicrobials – aminoglycosides are most effective (Gentamicin), doxycycline for bubonic form, fluoroquinolones and TMS are effective, chloramphenicol if CNS involved → treat for at least 21 days, tetracycline for 7 days for exposure
Prevention: flea control, prevent contact with burrows or carcasses of dead rodents and lagomorphs, minimize environmental attraction for rodents, high awareness in veterinary hospitals in endemic areas, no approved vaccines
Public health: highly contagious with about 25% of infected humans being veterinarians or technicians, 11% mortality in humans in US (late diagnosis increase fatality rate), advise owners to contact their physician

Tularemia

Etiology: Francisella tularensis, Gram – aerobic, encapsulated, coccobacillus with cell tropism for macrophages
Prevalence: occur in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere and all US states except Hawaii – sporadic in MN in spring and early summer
Transmission: predation of infected rodents, tick bites from Dermacenter spp. and Amblyomma americanum, biting flies
Pathogenesis: cutaneous inoculation → 1-5 day incubation period → replicates locally as an ulcerated papule → local lymph node dissemination (lymphadenopathy) → systemic spread to reticuloendothelial organs leading to sepsis and inflammatory responses with necrosis
Four clinical forms: ulceroglandular (localized infection of the skin and draining lymph nodes), typhoidal (severe systemic infection), oropharyngeal (involving the oral cavity), pneumonic (affecting the lungs)
Clinical signs: typically in cats (dogs less clinical) – acute onset high fever, lethargy, inappetence, peripheral +/- lymphadenopathy, lingual ulceration, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, +/- ulcerated cutaneous papule
Diagnosis: consider in cats and dogs with lymphadenopathy and high fever with access to outdoors and especially with history of predation → reportable disease (contact Board of Animal Health)
Culture – requires VSL 3 laboratory
PCR
Serology – acute and convalescent (3 weeks) may be needed
Treatment: antibiotics for 2-3 weeks (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides), supportive care with IV fluids and antinausea medications
Prognosis: life-threatening disease and early diagnosis and treatment is required for survival → many cats diagnosed postmortem
Public health: reportable disease in MN, BSL 3 organism, risk to owner and veterinary staff

Systemic Mycoses

Blastomycosis
Agent: Blastomyces dermatitidis lives as a mycelial form in the environment that generates infective spores in which the spores transform into yeast at body temperature when inhaled → big (5-20 um), blue, budding yeast with thick refractile, double contoured wall and broad-based bud
Found near water and sandy soil, by decaying wood, animal waste, excavation sites, and beaver dams
Transmission: inhalation of spores
Prevalence: affects dogs (young, healthy, large breeds) and humans → most common in the midwestern, mid-Atlantic and southeastern states (Ohio, Mississippi, Missouri, and St. Laurence River basins and Great Lakes region) → most common mycotic disease in MN and WI
Pathophysiology: incubation period of about 1-3 months (often mid to late summer/early fall) following inhalation → disseminates via vessels and lymphatics to eyes (40%), skin (25%), bone (25%), CNS, and other organs → asexual reproduction (budding) within the host → elicits pyogranulomatous (neutrophils and macrophages) inflammation
Manifestations – uveitis, conjunctivitis, chorioretinitis, optic neuritis, ulcerative skin lesions with serosanguineous to purulent drainage, >85% of cases have fungal pneumonia
Clinical signs: fever, anorexia, weight loss, lymphadenopathy
Diagnosis:
Imaging – diffuse, interstitial nodular infiltrate is most common pulmonary pattern on radiographs, but other patterns (alveolar, mass) are common and multiple patterns can co-exist
CBC – general inflammation → leukocytosis, mild non-regenerative anemia
Chemistry – general inflammation → hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia (acute phase protein changes) → rare hypercalcemia due to vitamin D activation by macrophages
Cytology – primary diagnostic → eg. lymph node aspirate
Blastomyces urine antigen (BUA) test – high sensitivity and specificity, great for respiratory cases when cytologic sampling is difficult, can be sued during monitoring and is possibly predictive of outcomes, results can take days to a week, cross reacts with Histoplasma
Serology – lower sensitivity
Biopsy – more invasive
Culture – lower sensitivity, slow growth, risky
Treatment: duration is long, at least 60 days but most require 6+ months with treatment lasting one month beyond resolution of detectable disease → discontinue treatment when clinical disease is gone and BUA is negative or weak + without further decline
Itraconazole 5 mg/kg SID (sometimes BID for first 1-2 weeks) → use microencapsulated and give with food and do not give with antacids → expensive therapy
Fluconazole is an alternative treatment with greater penetration into the CNS, eyes, and urogenital tract → cheaper, fewer side effects, but unclear if it works as well
Primary adverse effects of azoles – GI toxicity (anorexia, vomiting), hepatic toxicosis (increased ALT), cutaneous reactions (dermatitis, vasculitis)
Amphotericin B is an alternative treatment but is not first line treatment due to nephrotoxicity and potential for thrombophlebitis and anaphylaxis → reserved for severe cases failing to respond to azoles or with toxicities → intensive treatment with IVF/diuresis given during treatment (3x weekly for 4 weeks)
Anti-inflammatory used as adjunctive therapy – short course of NSAID or inflammatory dose of glucocorticoids can help with fever and pain
Prognosis: poor to fair with a 15-25% mortality – negative prognostic indicators include severe pulmonary involvement, CNS involvement, or more than 3 body systems affected → about 20% relapse in the first year
Zoonotic: reportable disease due to risk to humans and is an environmental source; direct animal-human transmission is rare
Histoplasmosis
Agent: Histoplasma capsulatum lives as a mycelial form in the environment that generates infective spores in which the spores transform into yeast at body temperature when inhaled or ingested and is phagocytized by mononuclear cells (intranuclear) → small, round, intracellular yeast (2-4 um) with round basophilic center and thin clear halo
Found in soil in warm, moist, humid regions with bird and bat feces being a common source
Transmission: inhalation or ingestion of spores
Prevalence: affects dogs, cats, and humans → most common systemic mycotic infection in North America and prevalent in temperate and tropical climates worldwide – Ohio, Mississippi, and Missouri Rivers
Increased risk seen in large breeds (Pointers, Weimaraners, Brittany Spaniels), young dogs and cats, and immunocompromised individuals
Pathophysiology: incubation period of about 2 weeks after inhalation or ingestion → disseminates via vessels and lymphatics with marked predilection for macrophages and most often affecting lungs and intestines → elicits pyogranulomatous (neutrophils and macrophages) inflammation
Clinical signs: subclinical infections are common but can cause large or small bowel disease, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly → results in diarrhea, melena, hematochezia, PLE, weight loss with uncommon signs being bone, ocular, dermal lesions, CNS involvement, oral lesions in cats, and diarrhea in cats
Diagnosis:
Imaging – diffuse interstitial miliary to nodular infiltrate appearance on thoracic radiographs (fungal pneumonia pattern is referred to as the "snowstorm effect")
Endoscopy – thickened, friable, hemorrhagic, or ulcerated intestines
CBC – general inflammation (leukocytosis, mild non-regenerative anemia) and uncommonly bone marrow disease (leukocytopenia, severe anemia, thrombocytopenia)
Chemistry – general inflammation → hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia (acute phase protein changes) → uncommonly hypercalcemia and elevated liver enzymes
Cytology – primary diagnostic → LN, liver, spleen, lung, bone marrow aspirates and rectal scraping
Histoplasma urine antigen (HUA) test – high sensitivity and specificity but cross reacts with Blastomyces
Serology – lower sensitivity
Biopsy – more invasive
Culture – lower sensitivity, slow growth, risky
Treatment: duration is long, at least 60 days but most require 4-6 months with treatment lasting one month beyond resolution of detectable disease
Itraconazole (5-10 mg/kg SID-BID) is drug of choice for treatment, but Amphotericin B can be concurrently for severe disseminated disease
Glucocorticoids are NOT recommended as immunosuppression increases risk for dissemination
Prognosis: poor to fair with a 25% mortality rate as nearly all survive to hospital discharge, but difficult to cure – negative prognostic indicators include CNS, ocular or boney involvement and positive prognostic indicator includes localized respiratory infection
Coccidioidomycosis (Valley Fever)
Agent: Coccidioides immitis lives as a mycelial form in the environment that generates infective spores which converts into a spherule when inhaled and exposed to carbon dioxide → large, non-budding spherules are 20-200 um in size and are endospore-filled
Prevalence: affects dogs and humans > cats → found in hot, semi-arid regions with sandy, alkaline soil in the southwestern US → epidemics can occur after dust storms or earthquakes
Transmission: inhalation or rare inoculation of spores
Pathophysiology: incubation period of 1-3 weeks after inhalation of spores → commonly affects lungs, bones, eyes, heart but can also affect testes, brain, spinal cord, visceral organs, or skin → elicits pyogranulomatous (neutrophils + macrophages) inflammation
Disseminated if spread beyond hilar lymph nodes with bones being a common site (65%)
Clinical signs: most cases are subclinical or mild respiratory, but dissemination can occur with pregnancy, immunosuppression, or massive exposure
Fungal pneumonia → results in cough, fever, weight loss, anorexia, more pyogenic than other mycotic diseases
Osteomyelitis has combination of lysis and new bone production
Skin lesions (masses, abscesses, local lymphadenopathy) are most common clinical signs in cats due to direct inoculation → cats can also have respiratory, ocular, or CNS diseases
Diagnosis: laboratory findings are often absent or consistent with mild general inflammation = Coccidioides is difficult to find as it is rarely seen with cytology or histology and may be hidden by neutrophils
Imaging – diffuse interstitial pattern is most common but other patterns include alveolar, miliary, and nodular on thoracic radiographs
Cytology – poor sensitivity
Antigen tests – poor sensitivity of <20%
Serology – most common diagnostic test → IgM titers are positive by about 1 week but negative by 2-4 weeks (can become positive again later with dissemination or recrudescence); IgG titers are positive by 4-6 weeks and are negative during acute infection or in immunocompromised individuals → low titers (<1:16) indicate early or previous infection while high titers (>1:32) indicate severe or disseminated disease but CNS infection is an exception in which titers are variable → consider repeating IgG titers in 2-4 weeks to monitor for increase and cats can have positive titers for a long time
Culture – not useful
PCR – might be an option but not widely available
Treatment: azoles – fluconazole, itraconazole or ketoconazole (5mg/kg BID) → treated for at 12 months or may require lifelong therapy due to frequent relapses off therapy
Prognosis: good for respiratory infections, but poor to fair for disseminated disease as it is difficult to cure
Cryptococcosis
Agent: Cryptococcus neoformans and Cryptococcus gatti species complex have filamentous forms in lab settings but environmental forms are unconfirmed→ young to oval yeast with narrow-base budding, thick capsule, and 4-7 um in size with capsule up to 30 um
Transmission: suspected inhalation
Prevalence: most common systemic mycosis of cats with young animals being highest at risk but can occur in dogs → ubiquitous (world-wide distribution) with bird droppings (especially pigeon) being a common source
Clinical signs: most cases are upper respiratory/nasal in cats → resulting in nasal or facial deformity ("Roman nose"), sneezing, nasal discharge, epistaxis, mass-like lesions protruding from nares, cutaneous lesions → ocular and CNS signs occur in 15% of cases and pulmonary involvement is uncommon
Clinical signs in dogs – CNS > eyes > nose → results in seizures, vestibular, mentation changes and uncommon signs including cutaneous lesions, fever, lymphadenopathy, disseminated disease
Diagnosis: typically lack CBC and chemistry panel abnormalities
CSF – increased protein, mixed neutrophilic and mononuclear pleocytosis, crypto organisms
Cytology – most often diagnosed by cytology using aspirations and impression smear samples of lesions
Latex cryptococcal antigen agglutination test – important for diagnosis → performed on serum or CSF with 98% sensitivity and positive at 1:16 or greater → titers are important for monitoring and should decrease 2- to 4-fold/month with decrease of 10-fold over 2 weeks being a favorable prognostic indicator – persistent titer indicates lack of response or relapse
Culture – rarely performed as takes 6 weeks
Treatment: treat cases until titer is negative with is 6-18 months on average and forever in FeLV+ cats → itraconazole is preferred treatment but fluconazole can be given for CNS disease or if side effects develop on itraconazole or amphotericin B if azoles fail
Prognosis: generally good to excellent but the exception is CNS disease which has a guarded prognosis
Aspergillosis
Agent: Aspergillus terreus, Aspergillus deflectus, Aspergillus flavipes, and Aspergillus fumigatus are ubiquitous saprophytic fungi that are dichotomously branching fungal hyphae
Transmission: inhalation
Prevalence: German Shepherd Dogs are greatest at risk and immunosuppressed are also at increased risk (breed predisposition is critical)
Pathophysiology: disseminated via hematogenous spread after inhalation to cause multiorgan infiltrate (distinct from nasal aspergillosis)
Diagnosis: can be difficult and requires multiple tests
Cytology, serology (some false negatives, culture
Serum and urine antigen test – false negatives with localized pulmonary disease and false positives with other systemic mycoses
Treatment: azoles and/or amphotericin B → newer generation azoles such as posaconazole might improve survival times
Prognosis: generally considered grave with survival range of 0-25 months and cure is rare (<10%)

Systemic Protozoal Diseases

Protozoan are small, sing-cell eukaryotes that are larger than bacteria

Alternating life stages – trophozoites actively feed and grow, cysts are dormant and are resistant to extreme temps/chemicals/lack of nutrition allowing survival outside of host
Coccidia
Neospora caninum
Agent: obligate intracellular protozoa within muscle and neuronal cells
Transmission: ingestion, placenta
Lifecycle: dog ingests tissue cysts in muscle tissue of intermediate host → bradyzoites released in dog duodenum → unsporulated oocyst passed in feces → sporulated oocysts in food, water, or soil → sporulated oocysts ingested by intermediate hosts
Dog is definitive host (natural infection is not reported in cats)
3 stages – oocysts shed in dog feces, tissue cysts with bradyzoites in intermediate hosts (muscle and neuro tissue only), tachyzoites replicate after acute infection
Clinical signs: most severe in puppies <6 months with transplacental infections manifesting from 4 weeks – 6 months → neuro and muscular signs predominate → ascending paralysis, rigid contracture, atrophy with progression to weakness, megaesophagus and death but are typically alert or mentally appropriate
Adult dogs – reactivation from chronic infection with multifocal CNS involvement → polymyositis or meningoencephalitis especially cerebellar (ataxia, circling, head tilt, nystagmus, tremors, seizures), occasionally myocarditis, dermatitis, pneumonia, hepatitis, also risk for death
Diagnosis:
Chemistry – elevated CK and AST if muscle involved, elevated ALT and ALP if liver involved
CSF – can help with brain signs and can compare antibody titers in CSF vs. serum, can perform PCR, normal to marked inflammation
Fecal – oocysts are rare
Serology – positive 2-3 weeks post-infection with titer > 1:50 IgG but may need to do paired titers → test pups after 32 days of life and no serologic cross reaction
Cytology or histopathology – tachyzoites similar to Toxoplasma, tissue cysts primarily in neural tissue
Treatment: clindamycin (10-12 mg/kg PO TID x at least 4 weeks) or trimethoprim-sulfa (15 mg/kg PO BID x at least 4 weeks); ponazuril is another option (20 mg/kg PO SID x at least 4 weeks) → treatment response can be partial or temporary and over 8 weeks may be needed → avoid immunosuppression → physiotherapy for those with polyradiculoneuritis-myositis
Treat all pups in litter
Prevention: cook meat and don't let dogs defecate in or around feed pens or livestock
Toxoplasma gondii
Agent: obligate intracellular protozoa
Transmission: ingestion, placenta, milk
Lifecyle: cat eats animal infected with bradyzoite cysts or ingests oocysts → bradyzoites or sporozoites released in cat intestine → unsporulated oocyst passed in feces → sporulated oocyst in food, water, or soil → sporulated oocysts ingested by intermediate hosts where sporozoites invade tissues and develop to tachyzoites → bradyzoites in cysts
3 stages – oocysts with sporozoites, rapidly dividing tachyzoites that disseminate throughout the host (cause clinical disease due to tachyzoite rapid intracellular replication causing rupture of cells), tissue cysts with bradyzoites remain for life
Cat is definitive host – produces oocysts, sheds for 2-3 weeks after infection, oocysts sporulate and are infectious in 3-5 days
Pathogenesis: spectrum of disease from subclinical to death due to tachyzoites depends on immunocompetence
Clinical signs in cats:
Kittens and immunocompromised – disseminated disease, reactivation via rupture of tissue cysts, overwhelming tachyzoite replication in lungs, CNS, eyes, liver, muscle, pancreas, heart, etc. → results in fever, diarrhea, dyspnea, icterus, seizures, death (esp. in neonates)
Adult cat – self-limiting diarrhea, uveitis, chorioretinitis, no signs are common (even shedding)
Clinical signs in dogs:
Neuromuscular form – long clinical course similar to Neospora and signs depend on site of location → seizures, ataxia, muscle wasting, paralysis
Disseminated form – most common in puppies with development of respiratory, hepatic and gastrointestinal signs → fever, diarrhea, vomiting, respiratory distress, icterus
Diagnosis:
CBC – non-specific leukocytosis, neutrophilia or neutropenia, lymphocytosis, monocytosis, eosinophilia
Chemistry – hyperproteinemia, hyperbilirubinemia, increased ALT (liver damage), increased ALP (cholestasis), increased CK and AST (muscle damage enzymes), hyperglobulinemia (polyclonal gammopathy in chronic dogs)
Imaging – interstitial to alveolar lung pattern on radiographs, hepatitis or pancreatitis on abdominal ultrasound, mass lesions in brain or spinal cord on MRI
Serology – most frequently used test → IgM associated with recent infection with positive results within 24 weeks and negative within 16 weeks but can be positive long if FIV+, immunosuppressed or ocular involvement; IgG is detected 3-4 weeks after infection and can remain positive for year (if IgM negative, need acute and convalescent IgG)
Histology – pyogranulomatous inflammation and necrosis, tachyzoites or tissues cysts (bradyzoites)
Fecal flotation – not useful for diagnosis since window of shedding is short but is useful to determine if cat is shedding → fecal PCR may be useful
Treatment: clindamycin (10-12 mg/kg PO BID x 4 weeks) or trimethoprim-sulfa (15 mg/kg PO BID x 4 weeks); azithromycin is another option (10 mg/kg PO SID x 4 weeks) → start with one antibiotic and if no significant clinical response within one week, try the other → adjunctive care includes supportive therapy and topical glucocorticoids with ocular involvement
Prognosis: drugs cannot eliminate infection so recurrence is possible especially with immunosuppression → prognosis poor if hepatic, CNS, pulmonary disease from tachyzoite replication and CNS signs may not completely resolve
Prevention: avoid feeding raw meat and prevent cats from roaming and hunting
Zoonotic: clean litter boxes every 24 hours, cook meat, wash vegetables, don't eat dirt, beware of sandboxes
Babesia
Agent: intracellular parasite of RBCs, Babesia canis (large piroplasm) and Babesia gibsoni (small piroplasm) infect dogs and Babesia felis uncommonly infects cats in Africa
Transmission: tick bite from Rhipicephalus sanguineus in USA transmits Babesia canis and Babesia gibsoni in which they must feed for 1-3 days for sporozoites in tick saliva to be injected into dog to attach to RBCs and become engulfed by endocytosis rupturing the RBCs; direct transmission between dogs via dog fighting transmits Babesia gibsoni
Predisposing factors: 60% of Babesia canis cases are Greyhounds which poses a risk to other breeds via transfusions, 93% of Babesia gibsoni cases are Bully breeds which poses risk to other breeds via dog bites, splenectomy
Pathophysiology: parasite incorporated into RBCs → parasite antigens incorporated into RBC membrane induce antibodies that attach to RBC membrane (spherocytosis) → RBCs cleared by mononuclear phagocyte system in spleen → antibodies may attach to platelets and non-infected RBCs (anemia and thrombocytopenia) and parasitic infection causes lipid peroxidation of RBC membrane leading to sludging in capillary beds (esp. CNS and muscle)
Clinical signs: may dogs are subclinical but can develop lethargy, depression, fever, splenomegaly, pallor and icterus, hemoglobinuria, and even death
Differentials: hemolytic anemia → IMHA, zinc toxicity, pyruvate kinase and phosphofructokinase deficiencies
Diagnosis:
Cytology – difficult to find but can examine feathered edge of smear using capillary blood
Serology – recommend acute and convalescent as species can cross-react
PCR – very sensitive, may identify subclinical infections → false negatives (low burden)
Treatment:
Babesia canis – imidocarb IM injection once and repeat in 14 days – good response rate and likely curative as it causes DNA damage, inhibits cell repair but side effects include salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation from anticholinesterase activity
Babesia gibsoni – imidocarb does not cure → azithromycin + atovaquone for 10 days is preferred but can use clindamycin + doxycycline + metronidazole for 3 months → supportive care with blood transfusions, IV fluids and treatment for secondary IMHA if needed
Prevention: tick control, always screen blood donors and screen thrombocytopenic dogs prior to splenectomy
Cytauxzoon felis
Agent: infects feline RBCs (piroplasms) and mononuclear cells
Transmission: tick bite from Amblyomma americanum
Pathophysiology: two stages
Schizont-laden macrophages – schizonts transferred during tick feeding get taken up by macrophages → replicate in macrophages which become large and occlude blood vessels especially in lung, liver, spleen, lymph nodes leading to multi-organ failure and DIC → rupture and release merozoites
Merozoites (piroplasms) infect RBCs
Diagnosis: CBC (anemia), chemistry (hyperbilirubinemia)
Cytology – blood smear to ID piroplasms or schizont-laden macrophages
PCR – sensitive with few days of turn-around time so many cats die before then
Treatment: atovaquone + azithromycin (60% survival) with supportive care of IV fluids, transfusion, heparin (to prevent DIC) → fatal if not treated early and aggressively
Hepatozoon canis
Agent: infects mononuclear and bone marrow cells, spleen, and lymph nodes
Transmission: ingestion of Rhipicephalus sanguineus tick (Brown dog tick)
Prevalence: Southeast United States and worldwide
Lifecycle: dog ingests tick → sporozoites released in dogs GI tract → merozoites form meronts or macromeronts → macromeronts form in hemolymphatic organs and elongated micromerozoites form within "wheel spoke" meronts → micromerozoites invade neutrophils → tick ingests dog blood → gametogenesis and fertilization occur in tick → mature oocyst forms → dog ingests tick
Clinical signs: most dogs are subclinical or develop mild infection – immunocompromised can develop lifethreatening disease, anemia fever, anorexia, lethargy
Diagnosis:
CBC – search for gamonts on blood smear
PCR
Treatment: imidocarb every 14 days until gamonts are gone

Immune-Mediated Hemolytic Anemia

disease in which autoantibodies are directed at antigens on the erythrocyte membrane, promoting their accelerated destruction– immune system misidentifies RBCs as foreign

Pathophysiology: type II hypersensitivity reaction (antibody-mediated immune reaction in which antibodies are directed against cellular or extracellular matrix antigens, resulting in cellular destruction, functional loss, or tissue damage)
Extravascular hemolysis – antibody or antibody and compliment coated RBCs phagocytized by macrophages in spleen → IgG more common, spherocytosis, hyperbilirubinemia, hyperbilirubinuria
Intravascular hemolysis – complement mediated lysis (membrane attack complex) → IgM more common, ghost cells, hemoglobinemia, hemoglobinuria, hyperbilirubinemia, hyperbilirubinuria
Clinical signs: non-specific (anorexia, lethargy, weakness), fever (immune-mediated inflammation), tachypnea/tachycardia (anemia, hypoxemia), pallor (anemia), icterus (RBC destruction and hemoglobin), splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, discolored urine/stool
Suspicion of IMHA: physical exam findings, signalment (dogs and cats), breed predisposition (many such as Cocker Spaniels, "little white fluffies", Poodles), any age (median 6 years in dogs), females over-represented, any age, any breed, any sex
Diagnosis:
Anemia - +/- regenerative (about 30% lack regeneration, or are pre-regenerative)
Hemolysis – at least one – hyperbilirubinemia, hemoglobinuria (intravascular hemolysis), hemoglobinuria (intravascular hemolysis), ghost cells (intravascular hemolysis)
Immune-mediated RBC destruction – at least two – agglutination (1:4 saline dilution), spherocytes (dogs), anti-RBC antibodies (DAT, flow cytometry, etc.) – or saline washed (3 times) RBC autoagglutination
Direct Coomb's test (DAT, direct antiglobulin test) if agglutination and spherocytosis are NOT both present
Classification:
Non-associative – primary or idiopathic
Associative – secondary → infections (Babesia gibsoni in dogs, Babesia felis in cats, Mycoplasma hemofelis in cats, FeLV in cats?), cancer?, drugs? (cefazedone in dogs, propylthiouracil in cats), vaccines?
Rational work-up: CBC with slide review for spherocytosis (evaluation of severity of anemia, presence of regeneration), slide agglutination, Direct Coomb's if needed, chemistry (degree of hyperbilirubinemia, other comorbidities), infectious disease testing (PCR for Babesia and Mycoplasma haemofelis), imaging (often low yield but used for neoplasia detection, blood type and crossmatch
Treatment:
Specific – immunosuppression to stop RBC immune destruction
Steroid – prednisone or prednisolone (cats) 2 mg/kg/day or if >25 kg 30-40 mg/m2/day, dexamethasone IV in hospital → when hematocrit >30% for 2 weeks and absence of agglutination, spherocytosis, resolution of hyperbilirubinemia, reduce steroid dose by 25% and continue gradual wean about 25% every 3 weeks with total treatment time often being 3-6 months
Steroid side effects include PU/PD, panting, muscle wasting, joint/tendon laxity (cruciate tear risk?), GI upset (gastric ulceration at high doses), behavior changes (depression, lethargy, aggression), insulin resistance (especially cats), heart failure (cats with underlying heart disease
Secondary agents – use in severe, life-threatening disease, lack of control of IMHA in 7 days with glucocorticoids alone, drop in PCV >5% in 24 hours, or reduce adverse effects of glucocorticoids → azathioprine 2 mg/kg/day reduced to q48hr after 2 weeks, cyclosporine 5 mg/kg BID, mycophenolate 8-12 mg/kg BID ***never use more than 2 immunosuppressives at once!***
Supportive – minimize tissue hypoxia by giving RBC transfusion (agglutination = crossmatching challenging) – give RBC transfusion when clinical for anemia (tachycardia, tachypnea, weakness)
Symptomatic – prevent thrombosis (risk of thrombosis is increased with IMHA) and minimize steroid side effects
Antithrombotics – thromboprophylaxis is indicated for all IMHA dogs unless platelets <30K (actively bleeding) as thrombosis in IMHA can occur from endothelial activation, intravascular tissue factor expression, procoagulant microparticle generation, platelet activation, or imbalance or pro- and anticoagulant factors → continue until remission and discontinuation of glucocorticoids
Anticoagulants first 2 weeks (low molecular weight heparin (eg. enoxaparin), factor Xa inhibitor (eg. rivaroxaban)) and antiplatelets (clopidogrel 1.1-4 mg/kg/day)
Prognosis: major cause of death in first month is thrombosis – 74% survival to discharge, 68% survival to 30 days – factors associated with decreased survival include ASA > 3, tbili > 5, azotemia

Immune-Mediated Thrombocytopenia (ITP)

Clinical signs associated with bleeding disorders:
Primary hemostatic defect (platelets) – bleeding from mucosal surfaces (epistaxis, melena, hematochezia, bleeding from gums, hematuria), bleeding from small vessels (petechiae/ecchymoses)
Secondary hemostatic defect (coagulation factors) – bleeding from large vessels (hemarthrosis, hemothorax, hemoperitoneum, hemoretroperitoneum, hematomas
Causes for thrombocytopenia: consumption (bleeding, neoplasia, DIC), decreased production (bone marrow disease), destruction (immune-mediated, infection), loss (rodenticide, neoplasia), sequestration (splenomegaly, neoplasia)
Pathophysiology: not well elucidated – T helper cells promote anti-platelet antibody generation → antibodies coat platelets, leading to clearance by reticuloendothelial system → macrophages then present antigen, continuing cycle → cytotoxic T cells can destroy platelets in blood or spleen → megakaryocytes in bone marrow can also be targeted
Degree of thrombocytopenia is key – <50,000 platelets/ul but often primary ITP is <20,000 platelets/ul (<10,000 platelets/ul is common)
Chief complaints for ITP: lethargy > decreased appetite/anorexia > melena > hematochezia, hematuria > epistaxis > hematemesis > hemoptysis, bruising, weakness > vomiting > increased respiratory effort > scleral hemorrhage, lameness, diarrhea > asymptomatic
Characterization of ITP:
Primary ITP (autoimmune, idiopathic, non-associative) – most common as any dog can get ITP, but increased incidence in middle-aged female dogs, Cocker Spaniels, Old English Sheepdogs, German Shepherd Dogs, and Poodles
Secondary TIP (associative) – infections (Anaplasma, Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever, Babesia), drugs (cephalosporins, sulfonamides), neoplasia, vaccination?
Antiplatelet antibodies and infectious disease – Anaplasma phagocytophilum, Ehrlichia canis, Babesia gibsoni, Leishmania infantum
Diagnosis: exclude other causes of thrombocytopenia as no test accurately demonstrates immune destruction but severity of thrombocytopenia (<50,000, often less than 10,000) is indicative
Antiplatelet antibody positive test result does not mean primary ITP and false negatives are possible if therapy has already been started
Evaluate for secondary causes of ITP – through history (drugs, vx, infectious disease exposure), look for neoplasia with chest and abdominal imaging (AUS, CXR vs. CT scan), infectious disease testing (4DxPlus, extended vector born disease panel depending on geography)
Treatment:
Eliminate underlying risk factors – treat infections/cancer, avoid immunogenic drugs, avoid vaccination?
Minimize bleeding – gentle handling, no jugular venipuncture, romiplostim? (thrombopoietin receptor agonist)
Immunosuppression – no change in dose until resolution of thrombocytopenia and stable for 2-4 weeks then slow dose reduction (25% every 2-4 weeks) and monitor platelet counts prior to reductions to monitor for relapse
Prednisone (prednisolone for cats) is mainstay of therapy – dogs 1-2 mg/kg/day or if >30 kg use 30-40 mg/m2/day, cats 2-4 mg/kg/day → often start a second immunosuppressive agent to allow for earlier or faster reductions in steroids
Second line agents – can be used as sole agents if steroid or contraindicated but may take longer to work → mycophenolate mofetil (16-24 mg/kg/day) or azathioprine (2 mg/kg/day x 1-2 weeks, then EOD) or cyclosporine modified (10 mg/kg/day)
Acute adjunctive therapy – vincristine, human intravenous immunoglobulin (more rapid increase in platelet count and decreased hospitalization time vs. prednisone alone)
Prognosis: most dogs will have increased platelet counts (>50,000 to 100,000) in 7-10 days with 89% surviving to discharge and 86% going into remission but recurrence rate is 26-47% → negative prognostic indicators include elevated BUN an melena

Immune-Mediated Polyarthritis (IMPA)

Immune-mediated non-erosive polyarthropathies: 2/3 of IMPA are primary, the other 1/3 have a trigger or underlying disorder

Types
Type I – idiopathic (65%)
Prevalence: middle aged large breed dogs
Physical exam: lameness or gait abnormalities (multiple limbs, single limb, shifting leg), fever, joint pain and/or swelling → can NOT diagnose idiopathic IMPA based on examination alone
Type II – associated with infection (UTI, endocarditis, other) (10%) → sterile joint fluid with positive culture elsewhere in body
Type III – hepatic or GI associated (4%)
Type IV – distant neoplasia (2%)
Misc categories – tick born disease-exposure vs. infection (50% +), SLE (8%), vaccine/drug induced (Akitas) (4%), early erosive (2%) → suspect if patient has IMPA and history of drug exposure < 30 days
Diagnostic workup for IMPA:
CBC – non-specific leukocytosis, mild anemia, identify cytopenias for SLE/tickborne disease, evaluate for organisms
Chemistry – mild non-specific (increased liver enzymes), identify organs for further evaluation
Urinalysis – identify proteinuria (Lyme disease, SLE), identify other organs for further evaluation
4Dx – tickborne disease
Radiographs – rule out erosive lesions
Histopathology – muscle for when polyarthritis and polymyositis are seen together
Arthrocentesis – sedate and prep aseptically → landmarks may be more challenging to isolate due to effusion → sample at least 3 joints with carpal and stifle joints being easiest to tap but tarsal joints have higher diagnostic yield → direct smear, cell counts, and bacterial cultures on synovial fluids
Synovial fluid analysis – increased fluids, decreased viscosity, fluid discoloration, nucleated cell count moderately to markedly increased (3,000-100,000/uL) with predominate cell type being NON-DEGENERATE NEUTROPHILS
Treatment for primary IMPA:
Immunosuppressants – prednisone (1-2 mg/kg/dog or if >30 kg then 30 mg/m2/day) or prednisolone (2-4 mg/kg/day) is mainstay of therapy but can use azathioprine, cyclosporine, leflunomide, or mycophenolate → repeat arthrocentesis and joint cytology every 2-3 weeks to guide therapy and add ancillary therapy in refractory patients at 2 weeks or relapsed patients or large breed dogs to avoid pred side effects
Prognosis for primary IMPA – >55% can be tapered of immunosuppressants, 20% managed with long term meds, 15% refractory to treatment and some recurrence can develop but is uncommon
Treatment for secondary IMPA:
Immunosuppressants? – not needed in many cases
Rickettsial – rapid improvement with doxycycline treatment of Anaplasma and Lyme with short term NSAIDs
Drug or vaccine induced – resolve after cessation of drug or with time but immunosuppressants may be needed
Distant neoplasia – identification and elimination if possible – lymphoma, osteosarcoma, hemangiosarcoma
Prognosis for secondary IMPA – dependent on underlying disease process but neoplastic process is poor prognosis

Immune-mediated erosive polyarthropathies

chronic, progressive inflammatory joint disease characterized by erosive and destructive changes within joints (AKA rheumatoid arthritis and feline chronic progressive polyarthritis)

Etiology – cellular and types II, III, IV hypersensitivity reactions present – synovitis likely from immune complex reactions (antigen-antibody) which include the rheumatoid factor (RF) to activate compliment cascade, stimulate prostaglandin release, and cause inflammation
Prevalence – small breed and medium breed dogs, middle aged (4-7 years), Greyhounds and Shelties are predisposed
Clinical signs – carpal, tarsal, interphalangeal joints most often affected – joint pain, effusion, soft tissue swelling, decreased ROM, systemic illness (fever, lymphadenopathy, anorexia)
Diagnostic work up – similar to primary IMPA
Synovial fluid analysis – nucleated cell count moderately to markedly increased (3,000-100,000/uL) with predominate cell type being more lymphocytes, culture negative, increased volume, discoloration, decreased viscosity
Rheumatoid factor serology – positive in 25-70% but can be positive in normal dogs
Radiographs – joint capsule distention, periarticular swelling, bony erosions and loss of trabecular bone at epiphyses
Histology – mononuclear cell inflammation of synovial tissue with villus hypertrophy and fibrin deposition – granulation tissue can extend from synovium to articular surface
Diagnosis typically based on radiographic and joint fluid findings and clinical signs!
Treatment – no specific therapy, pain management critical, steroids but can become refractory, combination immunosuppressive therapy may slow progression
Prognosis – poor as most animals are euthanized due to chronic pain from degenerative joint disease

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

multi-system immune disease

Etiology: unknown – primary immune disease or secondary disease due to underlying cause
Prevalence: rare in dogs and very rare in cats, middle aged to older, any breed but German Shepherd Dogs, Shelties, Collies,, and Poodles are overrepresented
Manifestations: polyarthropathy, skin lesions (often mucocutaneous junction), renal lesions (proteinuria, glomerulonephropathy), blood dyscrasia
Diagnosis: definitive diagnosis = positive ANA with at least 2 body systems affected and immunopath abnormalities in those tissues
Anti-nuclear antibody (serum ANA) – false positive and false negatives can occur
Immune disease – IMPA, IMHA, ITP, glomerular nephritis, polymyositis, immune mediated skin disease, immune mediated CNS disease
Treatment: immunosuppressive therapy – prednisone, azathioprine and others include cyclosporine, mycophenolate, cyclophosphamide
Prognosis: guarded, relapses can occur and long term therapy is needed in most

Transfusion Medicine

Transfusion Medicine

Goals of transfusion medicine:
1. Provide an increase in oxygen carrying capacity via red blood cell mass
2. Replenish deficient coagulation factors and hemostatic proteins
When to transfuse: increases in oxygen extraction from blood required to compensate for tissue oxygen demands occurs once PCV <15%
Clinical signs! – weakness, collapse, tachycardia, tachypnea (acutely anemic animals will have clinical signs at a higher PCV)
Compensatory mechanisms:
1. Sympathetic nervous system – diversion of blood away from skeletal, skin, splanchnic circulation and increase in heart rate and blood pressure
2. Increase in oxygen unloading at tissue level – decreased affinity of hemoglobin for oxygen
3. Increased renal erythropoietin production
Indications for plasma transfusion:
1. Fresh frozen plasma – coagulation factor deficiencies in the presence of active bleeding, DIC, hepatic failure, all hereditary coagulopathies, or massive hemorrhage
2. Frozen plasma – no labile clotting factors (F5, F8, vWF), rodenticide toxicity (vitamin K dependent factors), hemophilia B, or oncotic support?
3. Cryoprecipitate – portion of plasma that precipitates after fresh frozen plasma has been slowly thawed and is heavily concentrated with von Willebrand's factor and factor VIII, fibrinogen/fibronectin
4. Cryo-poor plasma – indicated for hemophilia B (F9), vitamin K deficiency, F11 deficiency, or F7 deficiency
Indications for platelet transfusion: uncontrolled or life-threatening bleeding due to thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopathia or massive transfusion
1. Fresh whole blood (<8 hours old) – massive hemorrhage, profound anemia with active bleeding, minimal platelet loss
2. Platelet rich plasma – prepared from soft spin of fresh whole blood but reduced platelet recovery and survival
3. Apheresis platelets, cryopreserved platelets in 6% DMSO
4. Lyophilized platelets – long shelf life and retains vWF adhesion function but short lifespan (minutes)
How much product:
1. Whole blood – 90 ml (dog) or 60 ml (cat) x kg x (PCVdesired – PCVpatient) / PCVdonor (assume donor PCV 60%)
2. PRBCs – 1.5 ml x kg x %PCV rise
3. FFP – 10-20 ml/kg or 15-30 ml/kg for vWB, hemophilia A
4. Platelets – 10 ml/kg fresh whole blood increases platelets by 10,000/uL or 1 unit platelet rich plasma/10 kg increases platelets by 40,000/uL
5. Cryoprecipitate – 1 unit / 10 kg
Pre-transfusion testing: blood type (canine DEA 1.1 antigen + or – and feline A, B, AB or Dal and Mik antigens), crossmatch (ALL cats and dogs who have received prior transfusion), and infectious disease testing for donors
Blood typing – tube agglutination (laboratory) or card agglutination (in-house) to test for DEA 1.1 and A, B, or AB
Canine blood types – dog erythrocyte antigens (DEA) 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 and Dal antigen → cell membrane on RBC has receptors that recognize the 8 blood groups → DEA 1.1 is most clinically significant and highly antigenic → dogs can tolerate one transfusion of non-type matched blood but universal donor is negative for DEA 1, 5, 7 and positive for DEA 4
Feline blood types – type A cats have low levels of anti-B antibodies, type B cats have high levels of antiA antibodies and type AB cats lack antibodies against type A or type B → cats also have Mik antigen so all cats need to be crossmatched → cats have naturally occurring alloantibodies so there is no universal feline donor
Crossmatch – detects incompatibilities not detected with blood typing – major crossmatch = donor RBCs with recipient plasma to detects recipient antibodies against donor RBCs – minor crossmatch = donor plasma with recipient RBCs
Transfusion administration:
Rate – 0.5-1 ml/kg/hr for first 15 minutes and after 15 minutes increase to 5-10 ml/kg/hr → deliver transfusion within 4 hours if tolerated
Method – dedicated line with standard blood filter set (170 microns), canine pRBCs should be administered through an in-line filter, use syringe pump with HemoNate (18 micron) filter for cats and small dogs <10 kg
Monitor – heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature every 15 minutes for a half hour, then every 30 minutes for 1 hours, then hourly during the remainder of the transfusion → monitor for signs of nausea, increased respiratory effort, changes in mentation, hematuria, and restlessness
Transfusion reactions: frequency of transfusion reactions of all types are generally low (3.3-15%) but febrile hemolytic reactions and anaphylaxis are most life threatening
Acute immunologic
Febrile non-hemolytic – pro-inflammatory cytokines from storage or infused antigen
Slow transfusion (diphenhydramine is ineffective as the reaction is secondary to inflammatory cytokines, not histamine
Febrile hemolytic – IgG mediated (dogs) or IgM (cats) mediated destruction of RBCs with most severe leading to hemoglobinemia, AKI, hypotension, shock, or DIC
STOP transfusion, renal support with IV fluids, FFP if DIC, supportive care of shock and hypotension (diphenhydramine ineffective as reaction is not mediated by histamine release)
Anaphylaxis – IgE mediated to plasma proteins and can be mild (urticaria, facial swelling) to severe (hypotension, shock)
Anaphylaxis = STOP transfusion, administer IV fluids and pressors (epinephrine) for acute phase and diphenhydramine and steroids for late phase
Allergic hypersensitivity reaction = stop transfusion for 30 minutes, treat (diphenhydramine) and restart at slower rate
Transfusion related acute lung injury (TRALI) – activated neutrophils damage pulmonary endothelium bilaterally within 6 hours with no evidence of CHF but resolves in 24-96 hours
Acute non-immunologic
Transfusion associated circulatory overload (TACO) – due to underlying cardiac disease or chronic anemia
Sepsis – results in marked fever, hypotension, tachycardia
Electrolyte derangements – hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, hypophosphatemia, hypocalcemia
Delayed immunologic
Delayed hemolytic – decreased RBC survival due to previous antibody exposure (canine DEA 3, 5, 7)
Transfusion related immune modulation (TRIM) – down regulation of cellular immune response
Delayed non-immunologic
Infectious disease transmission – eg. Mycoplasma, Babesia
Alternatives to blood products:
Autotransfusion – compatible and less immunosuppression but will not correct hypocoagulation
Synthetic colloids – cheap and readily available for acute blood pressure and oncotic support but prolonged use at higher dosages can cause platelet dysfunction and renal impairment
Antifibrinolytics – aminocaproic acid, tranexamic acid ideally within 6 hours of trauma for benefit – given to Greyhounds and those having undergone trauma or massive hemorrhage
